The Revenue Leak Hiding in Your Hotel's Phone Line

Missed Calls & Revenue · Published 2026-06-11 · Updated 2026-06-11 · By Kenny Patel

Ask a hotel owner yesterday's occupancy and you'll get a number to the decimal. Ask how many calls rang out yesterday and you'll get a shrug — not because owners don't care, but because no report anywhere shows it. The PMS doesn't log the booking that never happened. The channel manager doesn't flag the caller who gave up on hold. Missed calls are the only revenue leak in the building with no line item.

Why nobody tracks it

Everything else in hotel operations got instrumented over the last twenty years — rate, pace, RevPAR, review scores. The phone stayed analog. Calls arrive, someone answers or doesn't, and the evidence evaporates either way. When the month comes in soft, the explanations on offer are always the visible ones: the market, the OTA mix, the road construction. "We didn't answer the phone during the Tuesday check-in rush" isn't on the list, because nothing measures it.

The math, worked through

Here's the model on a plain 60-room property. Suppose the desk misses six calls a day — a conservative figure once you count overnight, rush hours, and breaks. Say a third of those are booking-related, which matches what most limited-service desks report about their inbound mix. If an answered booking call converts even a third of the time, and your ADR is $115 with a typical 1.5-night stay:

6 missed/day × 33% booking intent × 33% conversion × $172 booking value × 365 days ≈ $41,000 a year.

Every input there is adjustable, and you should adjust them — plug in your own ADR, your own call mix. But run it with deliberately pessimistic numbers and a mid-size property still lands five figures. And that's before the asymmetric misses: the one tournament coordinator or crew foreman whose 18-room block was on the other end of a call that rang out at 11:40pm.

When the calls actually get missed

Missed calls cluster in four predictable windows: overnight, when there's no desk coverage or the auditor is buried in the audit; the rush hours, 3–6pm check-in and 8–11am checkout, when the in-person line wins every time; the gaps — lunch, bathroom, walking a guest to the elevator; and hold-and-abandon, the calls that were technically answered and practically lost. If you only fix one, fix overnight: it has the highest booking intent per call of any window.

The 7-day self-audit

You don't need software to size your leak. For one week:

  1. Pull the unanswered-call count from your phone system or carrier portal, if it exists. Most show it; almost nobody looks.
  2. No portal? Keep a paper tally at the desk: every ring-out, every voicemail, every abandoned hold.
  3. Mystery-call your own property at 8am, 4pm, and 11pm each day. Log whether a human answered and how long it took.
  4. Multiply what you find by the model above, using your own ADR.

Most owners who run this audit stop arguing with the concept around day three.

Four ways to plug it

Add staff: solves it completely and costs the most — a loaded overnight position alone can exceed the entire leak. Forward to your cell: free, and it lasts until the third 1am breakfast question. A generic answering service: messages by morning, which for perishable booking calls is voicemail with better handwriting. A hotel-trained AI agent: answers every ring instantly, handles FAQs to completion, captures booking details, and escalates real emergencies — the approach we build at Anchor, and the one whose economics fit a lean property. The KPI worth adopting

Whatever you choose, start reporting one new number next to occupancy: answer rate — calls answered by a human or agent within three rings, divided by calls received. It's the cheapest KPI in the building to improve, and the only one currently rounding itself to "fine" without evidence.
