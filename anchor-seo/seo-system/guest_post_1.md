Walk into almost any hotel general manager's office and you'll find a dashboard. Occupancy. ADR. RevPAR. OTA commission spend. Labor cost per occupied room. Hoteliers measure nearly everything that touches revenue.

Here's a number almost nobody can tell you: how many phone calls went unanswered yesterday.

I've spent the past year talking with independent and franchise operators about their phone lines, and the pattern is remarkably consistent. Owners know calls get missed. They just have no idea how many, and they've never put a dollar figure on it. When they finally do, the number is usually uncomfortable.

## Why missed calls are invisible

Most hotel phone systems weren't built to report on failure. They log answered calls, maybe. They almost never surface the calls that rang out, hit a full voicemail box, or got abandoned after the fourth ring while your one front desk agent was processing a check-in, fielding a housekeeping question, and printing a folio.

There's also a psychological reason: a missed call doesn't feel like a loss. Nobody walked out. No refund was issued. The guest who couldn't reach you simply never appears in your data at all. They appear in your competitor's PMS instead.

## The math every operator should run

You don't need software to estimate the leak. You need four numbers and ten minutes.

1. **Daily inbound calls.** Pull this from your phone provider, or tally manually for a week. A typical 60- to 100-room limited-service property sees 20–60 inbound calls a day, more during local events.
2. **Miss rate.** Calls unanswered or abandoned. For lean-staffed properties, miss rates of 20–35% during peak hours and 60%+ overnight are common. Audit yours before trusting any vendor's claim — including mine.
3. **Booking intent.** What share of callers are trying to book or asking a pre-booking question (rates, availability, pets, late check-in)? Operators who track it usually land between 25% and 45%.
4. **Value per booking.** ADR multiplied by average length of stay.

Now a worked example for a 60-room property at $110 ADR and a 1.8-night average stay:

- 30 calls a day × 25% missed = 7.5 missed calls daily
- 7.5 × 35% booking intent = ~2.6 missed booking opportunities a day
- Assume only one in three of those would actually have converted: ~0.9 bookings a day
- 0.9 × ($110 × 1.8 nights) = **~$175 a day, or roughly $64,000 a year**

You can argue with any individual assumption — please do, with your own numbers. Halve everything and you're still looking at a five-figure annual leak at a property that would never tolerate a five-figure discrepancy anywhere else on the P&L.

And this counts only transient bookings. One missed group inquiry — a youth soccer tournament needing 18 rooms across a weekend, a construction crew booking six weeks — can outweigh a month of missed singles.

## When the calls actually get missed

Audit a week of call logs and three windows light up.

**Overnight.** Properties without a night auditor — or with one who's also doing laundry, security walks, and the breakfast prep list — miss the majority of calls between 11 p.m. and 7 a.m. These aren't junk calls. Late drivers deciding where to stop, West Coast travelers calling East Coast hotels, and anxious next-morning arrivals all call at night.

**The rush hours.** Check-in (3–7 p.m.) and check-out (7–10 a.m.) put your agent face-to-face with guests. The person standing at the desk wins; the phone loses. Ironically, your phones ring most exactly when your team is least able to answer.

**The single-coverage gaps.** Lunch breaks, bathroom breaks, the walk to room 214 to deliver towels. Solo coverage means the phone is unattended for a meaningful slice of every shift, and there's no schedule tweak that fixes it.

## A seven-day self-audit

Before buying anything or changing any process, run this:

- **Days 1–7:** Ask your phone provider for a call detail report — answered, missed, abandoned, voicemail, by hour. Most business lines can produce this; you may just never have asked.
- **Each evening:** Listen to every voicemail and log it: booking attempt, question, group inquiry, current-guest issue, junk.
- **Day 8:** Build the math above with your real numbers. Then ask one more question: of the booking-intent voicemails, how many did your team return within an hour? Speed matters — a caller who reached your voicemail is usually dialing the next property within minutes.

## What to do about it

There's no single right answer, but there are four honest options.

**Add labor.** A second agent during peaks and overnight coverage solves it completely — at $35,000–$45,000 per added full-time position. The math works for some properties; for most under 120 rooms it doesn't.

**Forward to a manager's cell.** Free, and how most small properties "solve" overnight today. It works until the manager is asleep, driving, or on vacation, and it quietly burns out your best people.

**A generic answering service.** Better than voicemail. But generalist operators reading from a thin script struggle with the questions hotel callers actually ask — rate guidance, pet policies, group blocks — and most can't distinguish a $4,000 group lead from a wrong number.

**Purpose-built coverage.** A newer category uses AI voice agents trained specifically on hotel operations to answer 24/7, handle the routine questions, capture structured booking and group leads, and transfer anything urgent to a human. If you evaluate this route, hold vendors to a hotel-specific standard: call their demo line, interrupt the agent mid-sentence, ask it something weird, and demand to see exactly how a group inquiry gets captured and routed. ## The KPI worth adopting

Whatever you choose, start measuring one new number weekly: **answer rate** — calls answered by a human or a competent agent within four rings, divided by total inbound calls. Put it on the same dashboard as occupancy and ADR.

Because here's the uncomfortable truth the dashboard hides: every caller has already found you, compared you, and chosen you. They did the hardest part of your marketing funnel for free. The only question left is whether anyone picks up.
